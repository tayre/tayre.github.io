01

What does “root” mean?

rootCthirdEfifthG

The root names the chord. C major and C minor both start with C as their root. You can repeat these notes at different pitches and still have the same chord.

02

What changes in a minor chord?

↓

E moves down one fret to E♭. C and G stay the same. That change in the third is what makes the chord minor. Play both to hear the difference.

03

Why learn more than one shape?

fret 5A+12 fretsfret 17A

Notes repeat along the neck. On the low E string, fret 5 is A and fret 17 is a higher A. New chord and scale shapes let you reach those other pitches without a big jump.

The minor pentatonic scale has five notes: A, C, D, E and G in the key of A minor. All five shapes are shown together on the fretboard below. Follow the notes along a string, or highlight a shape to see where it overlaps its neighbours.

“Pentatonic” means five notes. Start with A natural minor (A, B, C, D, E, F, G) and leave out B and F. The numbers above compare these notes with the A major scale: ♭3 and ♭7 mean its third and seventh notes are lowered by one fret.

    A phrase to try

    MOVE ALONG A STRING

    Take the B string through all five shapes

    Play E, G, A, C, D, E at the frets below. Let your hand move with the notes. This is one way to hear the scale as a line along the neck, instead of five separate patterns.

    01 / LEARN THE NOTES

    Find A before you run the pattern

    Play each red A, then choose one and add two nearby notes. All five shapes belong to A minor, even when their lowest note is C, D, E or G. Finding the root keeps you oriented.

    02 / JOIN TWO SHAPES

    Use the overlap

    Highlight a shape and its neighbour. The ringed notes belong to both patterns. Start a phrase in one shape, move through a shared note, then reach a new note in the next shape. Try the example below the fretboard.

    03 / MAKE SOME MUSIC

    Repeat a rhythm, change a note

    Play a short phrase, leave a pause, then answer it higher up the neck. Try ending on A, C or E over an Am chord. You can make a lot of music with a few notes and some space between them.

    Use the shapes in another key

    Move every note in every shape by the same number of frets. For G minor, move the A minor patterns two frets lower: shape 1 starts at fret 3 instead of 5, and the root becomes G. For B minor, move them two frets higher. The spacing between notes stays the same.

    Learn the shapes in small pieces. Spend time connecting 1 and 2 before adding 3, then keep going. Shape 5 also fits at frets 2–5, so you can connect it to shape 1 near the bottom of the neck.

    LESS PATTERN RUNNING. MORE PHRASING.

    Make the pentatonic sound like a solo.

    Start with A minor, shape 1 and a slow groove. Play a few notes, leave a gap, then answer yourself. You can play along on your guitar or tap the fretboard.

    YOUR FIRST MUSICAL SENTENCE

    Say something. Leave space.

    1. Play just the two thinnest strings for a minute. Use two or three notes at a time.
    2. Finish a phrase on a ringed note. Hold it for a beat and listen to it against the chord.
    3. Repeat your rhythm, change one note, then leave a whole bar of silence.

    A one-bar lick to borrow

    The labels give string, fret and beat. “&” is halfway between beats. In Listen & answer, hear a lick for one bar, then use the next bar to play your own answer.

    When it starts to feel comfortable

    Try a different shape, add a slide between two scale notes, or select the chord changes. The rings follow the backing chord: they show notes from your pentatonic scale that also belong to that chord. The other scale notes add movement; use your ear and resolve to a ringed note.

    This lesson uses minor pentatonic (1 · ♭3 · 4 · 5 · ♭7) over a minor chord or a compatible minor-key progression. A new song may need a different scale—match the key and harmony before using a pattern.
